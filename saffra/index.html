A real chef, not a generic chatbot

Powered by advanced AI — shaped into a real chef. Saffra has a name, a voice, signature moves, and hard safety rules. Her tone calibrates to your skill: beginners get sensory cues ("when oil shimmers, when it smells nutty"), pros get terse technique-first ("hit it with a nappé"). One model, infinite cooks.

A catalog written in advance — not improvised at the stove

No Saffra recipe is improvised the moment you ask. The catalog is written in advance and reviewed in-house, with allergen flags on every recipe — and it is written to be cooked from: 99.8% of ingredients carry a measured amount, 97.2% say how to prep it, every recipe lists the equipment it needs, and 99.3% name a cue for what done looks like. Saffra's catalog: 2,300+ recipes across 25+ cuisines, and every one of them is semantically searchable (vector embeddings). Then she adapts it to your pantry, your skill, your equipment.

Memory that compounds

Rolling conversation summaries. Cook history with star ratings. Live pantry awareness. Recent-suggestion tracking so she doesn't pitch the same dish twice. The longer you cook with her, the sharper she gets — leaning into what you loved, steering around what flopped, varying within genres you've succeeded at, never repeating last Tuesday's dinner unless you ask.

Nine specialized tools, used silently

Saffra doesn't just talk — she works. She has nine purpose-built tools: pantry search, recipe lookup, recipe adaptation, ingredient substitution, technique explainer, voice timers, unit conversion, recipe saving, profile updates. She picks the right tools for each turn and chains them — "what's for dinner?" might fire pantry-search → recipe-lookup → adapt-for-user, all silently before answering.

Allergen safety as a hard rule

Declared allergies are non-negotiable — hard refusal rules, not soft suggestions. She'll either swap loud and clear or pass on the recipe entirely. Cross-contamination flags for serious allergies (peanut, tree nut, shellfish, sesame, gluten-for-celiac). USDA-safe internal temperatures cited in every recipe that needs one.

A voice built for a real kitchen

Streaming neural TTS — Saffra's signature voice — served from a content-addressed cache so the same line never re-renders twice. Speech recognition uses Apple's Speech framework, which is server-backed — audio goes to Apple, not to us. Every question you speak in Cooking Mode is also re-transcribed by OpenAI's Whisper for accuracy, then discarded. The wake phrase "hey saffra" is only armed inside Cooking Mode, never app-wide. Voice timers land in Live Activities, including the Dynamic Island.

One step at a time.
Hands never touch the screen.

Tap "Let's cook" on any recipe and the whole phone becomes a chef at your shoulder. One step per screen, big enough to read from across the counter, with a photo of the dish, the exact ingredients this step needs, and a timer she's already sized for you.

  • She reads every step aloud

    Saffra narrates in her signature voice, then listens. "Hey saffra, next step" — "replay that" — "how long on the timer?" Your hands stay in the food.

  • Just the ingredients this step needs

    Each step card pulls out exactly what you're reaching for — "spaghetti, salt" — so you're never re-scanning the full list mid-sizzle.

  • Timers she's already sized

    "15 min — saffra suggests this timer." One tap (or one sentence) and it's running in the Dynamic Island, even if you leave the app.

  • Ask anything, mid-step

    "Why reserve the pasta water?" The ask-saffra bar answers inline without leaving the step — and a "coming up" preview keeps you one move ahead.

Your Kitchen

A pantry that fills itself —
and empties smart.

Saffra can only cook from your kitchen if she knows what's in it — so getting food into the app takes seconds, not typing. And once it's in, freshness is tracked: things about to turn get flagged and pitched for tonight's dinner instead of the trash.

  • Snap your fridge

    Photograph a shelf and she identifies what's on it. Items land categorized, no typing. This one photo is sent to our server to be read; receipts and cookbook pages are read on your phone.

  • Scan a receipt

    On-device OCR reads the lines, AI parses them into clean items — your photo never leaves the phone.

  • Or beep the barcode

    Lookup runs against Open Food Facts — items arrive pre-tagged with category, expiration, and allergen flags.

  • Expiring? It's dinner.

    Items going stale get flagged in the pantry and surfaced in chat — "the cilantro's going yellow, let's use it tonight."

Three differences matter. First: a general model drafts a recipe the moment you ask, from model knowledge alone. Saffra's flagship recipes are written in advance and complete enough to cook from — 99.8% of ingredients carry a measured amount, 97.2% say how to prep it, every recipe lists the equipment it needs, and 99.3% name a cue for what done looks like. Second: the assistant works with your pantry, equipment, allergies, skill level, and current cooking step rather than treating each question in isolation. Third: product-specific tools handle catalog search, substitutions, techniques, and hands-free cooking, so the model is one part of a larger cooking system.

The core assistant uses a multimodal AI model, with a faster utility model for tasks such as parsing receipt text and folding older conversations into summaries. Recipe search uses vector embeddings over the curated library. Voice synthesis uses a neural TTS voice with content-addressed caching, while speech recognition uses Apple's Speech framework, which is server-backed — the audio reaches Apple, not us. In Cooking Mode the clip is also re-transcribed by OpenAI's Whisper, for every spoken question rather than only noisy ones, then discarded. Receipt and cookbook OCR runs on-device through Apple's Vision framework; shelf photos are sent to the server for vision analysis and are not stored. The recipe catalog is written ahead of time rather than improvised per request, and every recipe carries allergen flags.

Two things keep her grounded. One: when you ask for a specific dish or "what should I cook?", she calls a tool that searches Saffra's own recipe catalog — every recipe in it written in advance — instead of improvising one from memory. So you get a real recipe, adapted for you, not a hallucination. Two: for techniques and substitutions she calls dedicated tools (technique explainer, substitution lookup) rather than guessing. She also won't claim to have personally tasted something — she'll say "by the numbers, this should read bright and savory" instead of "I love this." If she's not sure, she says so.
